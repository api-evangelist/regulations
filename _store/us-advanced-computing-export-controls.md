The **US advanced-computing export controls** are administered by the Bureau of Industry and Security
under the Export Administration Regulations, and they are the most operationally intrusive regime in
this catalog for the companies they touch. They restrict the movement of advanced computing
integrated circuits, semiconductor manufacturing equipment and associated technology, using technical
performance thresholds to define what is controlled.

  * **Performance thresholds** - Whether a chip is controlled depends on measurable characteristics, so specifications are compliance-relevant facts.
  * **End-use and end-user screening** - Obligations attach to the seller, including the Entity List and know-your-customer red flags.
  * **Extraterritorial reach** - The Foreign Direct Product Rule extends control to some goods made abroad with US technology.
  * **Frequently revised** - Thresholds and scope have been updated repeatedly since 2022.

This regime deserves careful handling in any assessment of semiconductor API practice, and *The State
of Compute & Hardware APIs* gives it exactly that. **It is the only regulation in this research that
gives a company a legitimate reason to publish less.** When performance characteristics are licensing
triggers and screening duties attach to whoever responds, a public, self-serve, machine-readable
surface enumerating capabilities and answering anonymously is a genuine compliance question rather
than a hypothetical one.

What it does not explain is the shape of the gap. Nothing in export control prevents a company from
publishing part lifecycle status, errata, a documented error envelope or an idempotency key — none of
which is controlled technology, and all of which are missing across the silicon layer.
